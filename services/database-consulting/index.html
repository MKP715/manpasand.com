What a SQL Server consultant does for a small business

Most small businesses don’t need a full-time database administrator. They need someone to find out why the invoicing screen crawls at month-end, confirm last night’s backup would really restore, and write down how it all works. That’s our work in SQL Server, PostgreSQL and MySQL, whether the database came with vendor software or your own developer built it. You can also read what two clients told us.

Tuning starts with measuring. In SQL Server that means Query Store, which records queries, plans and run-time statistics. It’s on by default only for new databases since SQL Server 2022, so older systems need it turned on first. PostgreSQL has pg_stat_statements; MySQL has its slow query log. The usual culprits are a missing or wrong index, a query that reads a whole table to show one row, or stale statistics. A long transaction can block everyone, and in PostgreSQL autovacuum can fall behind. We change one thing at a time, measure again and keep a script to undo each change. If the bottleneck is the server or the network, that’s small business IT support.

Some vendor software runs on the free SQL Server Express edition, which has hard limits. In SQL Server 2025, Express:

  • Caps each database at 50 GB (10 GB in SQL Server 2022 and 2019)
  • Uses at most one socket or 4 cores, and 1,410 MB of memory for its data cache
  • Has no SQL Server Agent, so backups don’t schedule themselves

When a database hits those limits, no index will fix it. The choices are archiving old data or moving to Standard edition, and we’ll price both.

Backups you’ve test-restored, not just scheduled

Microsoft’s documentation is blunt: “You don’t have a restore strategy until you test your backups.” A job that reports success only proves a file was written. Even RESTORE VERIFYONLY checks that a backup is complete and readable, not that the data inside is structurally sound. So we restore to a separate machine, run DBCC CHECKDB on the copy and time it, so you know how long you’d really be down.

The schedule follows two questions: how much work could you stand to re-enter, and how long can you go without the system? If losing an hour of orders would hurt, you need log backups every few minutes; a database that changes only on payday may need one nightly backup. In PostgreSQL the usual choice is nightly pg_dump files or continuous archiving for point-in-time recovery. One trap we check for is the full recovery model with no log backups. The log can’t be cleared and grows until it runs out of room. Then SQL Server lets you read the data but not update it.

Then a copy leaves the building. CISA’s backup guidance for small businesses recommends the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, on two types of storage, with one off-site. A USB drive plugged into the server shares its fate in a fire, theft or ransomware attack. North Texas has its own reminder. During Winter Storm Uri in February 2021, 69 percent of Texans lost power at some point, for an average of 42 hours, according to a University of Houston survey. A cloud copy can be restored elsewhere while the office is dark. Our guide to protecting office tech from power outages covers the UPS side.

Do you need a data warehouse, or just better reports?

Most small-business reporting trouble isn’t a missing warehouse. It’s a report that runs against the live system and slows everyone down, a sales number that changes depending on who pulls it, or a Monday spreadsheet someone assembles by hand from three exports.

Often the fix is modest: a few indexed reporting views, or a nightly copy of the tables your reports use, with the business rules (what counts as a sale, which fiscal calendar) written down once. That works for one or two source systems. When data comes from several systems that don’t share customer or product IDs, an ETL pipeline into a small warehouse of fact and dimension tables earns its extra build and upkeep. We’ll tell you which side of that line you’re on.

We build for the reporting tool you use, whether that’s Excel, Power BI or something else. Two Power BI facts are worth knowing. First, a free license only lets you build reports for yourself, so sharing with coworkers generally takes paid Pro or Premium Per User licenses. Second, a database on your own server is reached through Microsoft’s on-premises data gateway. That’s a small Windows program that connects outbound, so no inbound firewall ports get opened. We set up the data side. Which licenses to buy stays your call.

Access, confidentiality and what Texas law requires

Database work means seeing your data, so we keep that exposure small: a login with only the permissions the job needs, work on a copy when we can, and a reminder to disable our login when the project ends.

Texas law also gives us a duty to you. Under Business and Commerce Code §521.053(c), anyone who maintains computerized data containing sensitive personal information they don’t own has a duty to the owner. If they discover a breach in which that information was, or is reasonably believed to have been, acquired by someone unauthorized, they must notify the owner immediately. If we ever discover a breach in data we maintain for you, we’ll tell you right away. Then your clock runs. You notify affected people without unreasonable delay and within 60 days after you determine the breach occurred. If 250 or more Texans are involved, you also notify the Texas Attorney General within 30 days. Our breach notification guide has details; it’s general information, not legal advice.

The same chapter requires reasonable procedures to protect that information and the destruction of customer records containing it that you don’t need to keep (§521.052). Texas defines that information mainly as a name paired with an unencrypted Social Security number, driver’s license or government ID number, or card or account number with its access code. Health information tied to a person counts too. During a health check we look for those columns stored in plain text, and for old tables nobody uses, so you can decide what to encrypt, lock down or purge.

SQL database & ETL questions

All FAQs
Q.01Should a small business use SQL Server or PostgreSQL?

If your accounting, inventory or practice-management software requires SQL Server, use SQL Server, and the free Express edition is fine until you hit its limits. For a new custom application, PostgreSQL is free under a permissive open-source license with no editions to outgrow, while Microsoft’s estimated retail price for SQL Server 2025 Standard is $3,945 per two-core pack, with a four-core minimum per processor (or $989 per server plus $230 per user or device); your reseller sets the final price. MySQL Community Edition is also free and is what many web apps expect, WordPress among them, and our web development work covers the site side.

Q.02We’re still on SQL Server 2016. Is that a problem?

It’s a risk to plan around. Microsoft’s extended support for SQL Server 2016 ended in July 2026, so it gets no more security updates unless you pay for Extended Security Updates, and SQL Server 2017 follows in October 2027. The same goes for other databases: PostgreSQL 13 reached end of life in November 2025, PostgreSQL 14’s end-of-life date is November 12, 2026, and Oracle moved MySQL 8.0 to Sustaining Support in April 2026. Check which versions your software vendor supports, test the upgrade on a copy, then move; if the vendor’s answer is an expensive new license, get a second opinion on the quote.

Q.03How do I speed up a slow SQL Server database?

First pin down what’s slow: one screen, one report or everything, and all day or only while certain jobs run. Then check the basics: free disk space, whether the server is short on memory, and whether backups or maintenance overlap your busy hours. If one query or report is the problem, the fix is usually an index, a rewritten query or fresh statistics, and Query Store will show which queries cost the most. A bigger server can help, but measure first so you pay to fix the real bottleneck.

Q.04What is ETL, and does my business need it?

ETL stands for extract, transform, load: pulling data out of one system, cleaning it up and loading it somewhere else on a schedule. If someone on your team copies exports from two or three systems into a spreadsheet every week, that’s ETL done by hand, and automating it gives back those hours and removes the copy-paste mistakes. If your reports come from one system with decent built-in reporting, you probably don’t need it.

Q.05Can database consulting be done remotely?

Yes, most of it. We connect through your VPN or a remote-access tool you approve, with a login limited to what the job needs. We come on-site when the work involves server hardware, the office network, or people who would rather show us than tell us, anywhere in our Dallas–Fort Worth service area.

Q.06How much does database consulting cost?

It depends on the job: a one-time health check, a backup fix and a multi-source ETL build are very different amounts of work. After a free consultation, we send a written scope and a fixed-price quote before any work begins, with no hidden fees. If Texas sales tax applies to the work, it’s shown as its own line on the quote.
