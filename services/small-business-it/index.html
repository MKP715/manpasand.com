What small business IT support covers in a 2–25 person office

Most offices this size don’t need a full-time IT person. They need someone who can come in when the printer stops scanning to email, the Wi‑Fi drops in the back room, or a new hire starts Monday and needs a laptop and a login.

Older PCs need a decision now. Windows 10 security updates stopped on October 14, 2025, except for PCs in Microsoft’s paid Extended Security Updates program. For businesses that means up to three years at $61 per device for the first year, doubling each year, and a late sign-up pays for the years it missed. Windows 11 needs a TPM 2.0 chip and a supported processor, so some older machines can’t upgrade. We check each PC and tell you which can move to Windows 11, which are worth keeping patched for now, and which to replace.

In a shop or restaurant, the point-of-sale software usually belongs to a vendor, but it runs on your network. We set up and troubleshoot the part that’s yours: the router, the wired connections and the Wi‑Fi your card readers and receipt printers use. The PCI Security Standards Council’s guide for small merchants says that if you offer customers free Wi‑Fi, payments belong on a different network, and the device you take payments on shouldn’t be used for email or browsing. We set it up that way. For firewalls and segmented Wi‑Fi across a larger office, see network security for small businesses.

A few jobs have their own pages: conference room AV setup for meeting rooms, technology consulting when you want a plan or a second opinion before you buy, and SQL Server consulting when the trouble is a database.

What to have written down before something breaks

The hardest IT calls aren’t about broken hardware. They’re about a login nobody has: the person who set up email left years ago, or the router password was on a sticky note that got thrown out. On every job we write down what we find, in a record you keep. It should answer these questions:

  • Who has admin rights to Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace? Microsoft recommends fewer than five Global Administrators, each using multifactor authentication.
  • Where is your domain registered, in whose account, and when does it renew?
  • What’s your internet provider’s account number, and who has the router or firewall login?
  • Who uses each computer, printer and tablet, and when does its warranty end?
  • What remote-access tools has your POS provider installed? The PCI guide says to ask vendors how to switch remote access off when it isn’t needed.
  • Where do backups go, how often do they run, and when was the last test restore?

New hires, departures and backups: the jobs that get skipped

New hires get their own login for everything, with multifactor authentication on and passwords in a password manager the business controls, not a spreadsheet. The PCI guide says never to share logins on payment systems, and Microsoft says its studies show an account is 99.9% less likely to be compromised with multifactor authentication. There’s a legal angle too. Since September 1, 2025, a Texas business with fewer than 250 employees can’t be made to pay exemplary (punitive) damages in a data-breach lawsuit if it had a qualifying cybersecurity program in place when the breach happened. For businesses under 20 employees, that program includes password policies and staff security training. Our guide to Texas breach notification rules has the details.

When someone leaves, deleting the account is the step people rush, and it’s the one that loses data. Microsoft 365 can restore a deleted user for 30 days, Google Workspace for 20; after that, the mail and files are gone. In Microsoft 365, converting the mailbox to a shared mailbox keeps the email and calendar, and under 50 GB it needs no license, but the old password still works until someone resets it. In Google Workspace, transfer their Drive files and email to a coworker first; anything not transferred is deleted with the account.

CISA calls the 3-2-1 rule for backups a trusted guideline: three copies of important files, on two kinds of storage, one kept off-site. It also calls for scheduled recovery tests. A backup nobody has restored from is a guess, so we set backups to run on their own and restore a test file to prove they work.

Power is the other weak spot: a short outage can drop the modem, router and card readers mid-sale. Our guide to protecting office tech from power outages covers battery backups. If your POS can take cards offline, know its limits. Square, for example, expires pending offline payments after 72 hours and holds the seller responsible for any that expire, are declined or are disputed.

All FAQs
Q.01How much does IT support cost for a small business?

It depends on how it’s sold. Managed IT providers commonly charge a recurring monthly fee, often per device or per user, while break-fix support is paid for when something needs fixing. We don’t publish prices, because the work varies too much from one office to the next. After a free consultation you get a fixed-price quote, with any Texas sales tax on its own line, before any work starts.

Q.02What is the difference between managed IT and break-fix support?

Managed IT is a subscription: the provider watches your systems and handles a defined list of work for a recurring monthly fee. Break-fix means support happens after a problem shows up, and you pay for that job. A managed plan suits an office that wants someone monitoring every day. For a small office with a handful of PCs, paying for the work you actually need is often simpler.

Q.03Do I need a monthly IT contract?

Not every office does. Our small business IT support is on demand: you call when something needs doing, and we quote it at a fixed price before we start. If you want systems monitored every day, a managed-services contract is built for that, and we’ll tell you honestly whether an office your size needs one.

Q.04Can you set up and support our POS system?

We handle the network side: the router, the wired connections, the Wi‑Fi your card readers and receipt printers use, and keeping payments on a different network from customer Wi‑Fi, as the PCI Security Standards Council advises. Problems inside the POS software or with your merchant account go to your POS provider’s support line. When something stops working, we’ll tell you which side the problem is on.

Q.05What does small business IT support include?

For us it means the everyday technology a small office runs on: computers and printers, the office network and Wi‑Fi, the network side of your POS, Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace accounts, backups, new hires and departures, and a written record of all of it. It doesn’t include security cameras or alarm systems. Texas requires a DPS Private Security license for that work, and we don’t hold one. Hire a licensed company — you can check any installer on DPS’s license search.
