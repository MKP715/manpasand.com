What an IT consultant checks before you sign

A quote can look reasonable and still leave out what matters. Send us the proposal for a new office network, a phone system, a website redesign, a software rollout or a monthly IT plan, and we’ll read it line by line. Whatever you share with us stays confidential.

The FTC’s advice to small businesses fits in one line: put your security expectations in the contract, and don’t just take vendors at their word. What you get back is a plain list of what looks fair, what to question and what to ask before you sign. Sometimes the right call is the quote you already have, and we’ll say so. Here’s what we check:

  • Scope. Does each line say what will be done, to which equipment and by when, or just “network upgrade” and a number?
  • Equipment and licenses. Model numbers, quantities and seat counts that match your actual staff, and whether gear you already own could stay in service.
  • One-time vs. recurring costs. The three-year total, not just the first invoice, and whether each subscription is an annual commitment or month to month. Microsoft 365 Business Standard, for example, is $14 per user per month on an auto-renewing annual plan, or $16.80 month to month.
  • Contract terms. Length, auto-renewal, notice period, price-increase clauses and cancellation fees.
  • Ownership and exit. Who holds the admin passwords, the domain name and your data, and how you get all three back if you leave.
  • Security duties. CISA’s guidance for businesses that use IT providers says contracts should spell out the security controls you expect, monitoring of the provider’s access to your network, and notice when the provider has a security incident of its own.

Technology roadmaps and budgets, built on real dates

A roadmap starts with an inventory: every PC, laptop, server, router, firewall, printer and subscription, with its age, what it runs and when support for it ends. For an office we do that walk-through on-site anywhere in our Dallas–Fort Worth service area.

Then everything goes on one calendar with rough costs, so ten PCs don’t all come due in the same quarter and a renewal notice doesn’t arrive as a surprise. Microsoft publishes its support dates years ahead. These, plus one Texas law, are worth planning a 2027 budget around:

  • Windows 10 stopped getting free security updates on October 14, 2025. Businesses can buy Extended Security Updates for up to three more years, at a price that doubles each year (the math is below).
  • Office 2016 and 2019 reached end of support on October 14, 2025, and Office 2021 followed on October 13, 2026.
  • Windows Server 2016 reaches end of support on January 12, 2027.
  • SQL Server 2016 reached end of support on July 14, 2026, and SQL Server 2017 follows on October 12, 2027. If your business software runs on either, see our SQL Server consulting.
  • Texas law. Since September 1, 2025, a Texas business with fewer than 250 employees that keeps a qualifying cybersecurity program is shielded from exemplary (punitive) damages in a data-breach lawsuit. Our guide explains Texas’s 2025 cybersecurity safe harbor and the notice deadlines.

Choosing between options, in plain English

Most tech decisions come down to two or three sensible choices that cost different amounts over different stretches of time. We put them side by side, then tell you which one we’d pick for your situation and why. Three common ones:

  • Microsoft 365 or a one-time Office purchase. Business Standard costs $14 per user per month on an annual plan, about $504 per person over three years at today’s price, and includes business email and 1 TB of cloud storage per user. Office Home & Business 2024 costs $249.99 once for one PC or Mac and covers the desktop apps only, and its support ends in October 2029. Subscriptions also go up: Business Standard was $12.50 until July 1, 2026.
  • Upgrade, extend or replace a Windows 10 PC. If it meets Windows 11’s requirements, including TPM 2.0 and a compatible 64-bit processor, upgrading is the cheap route. If not, business Extended Security Updates cost $61 per PC for year one and double each year. Year one began in November 2025, and late buyers pay for the years they missed, so buying now costs $183 per PC for a stopgap that ends by October 2028. Weigh that against a replacement.
  • Keep the server or move to the cloud. A server in the closet is paid for once and keeps working when the internet is down, but its backups, power and eventual replacement are on you. Cloud services bill every month and need a dependable connection, but someone else maintains the hardware. A mix of the two is often the practical answer.

Second opinions for homes

Household tech decisions get the same treatment. Before you switch internet plans, search your address on the FCC National Broadband Map to see which providers report service there. Then compare their FCC broadband labels. Providers must post one for each plan, showing price, introductory rates, data allowances and speeds. A faster plan won’t fix weak Wi‑Fi in a back bedroom; that’s usually a router placement or mesh Wi‑Fi question.

If the family computer still runs Windows 10, Microsoft’s consumer Extended Security Updates run through October 12, 2027, at no cost if you sync your PC settings. That buys time to decide between upgrading and replacing. And if a call, text or pop-up says your computer has a virus, check with someone you trust before you pay anyone. As the FTC puts it, “Legitimate tech companies won’t contact you by phone, email, or text message to tell you there’s a problem with your computer.”

All FAQs
Q.01What does an IT consultant do for a small business?

An IT consultant helps you decide; IT support does the hands-on fixing. In practice that means reviewing what you have, checking quotes and contracts, planning replacements around support dates and explaining your options with their trade-offs. If you’d also like the work done, our on-demand IT help for your office covers that side.

Q.02How much does an IT consultant cost?

Consultants usually charge by the hour, by the project or with a monthly retainer. We don’t publish prices because the work varies too much: reading one quote is a different job from inventorying a 20-PC office. The first consultation is free, and anything beyond it gets a written scope and a fixed price before we start, with any Texas sales tax shown as its own line.

Q.03When should a small business hire an IT consultant?

Before money changes hands: ahead of a multi-year contract, a batch of new PCs, a switch to a new IT provider or new business software. A support deadline is another good trigger, such as Windows Server 2016 on January 12, 2027. If you’re in the middle of a security problem, start with network security help to contain it, restore systems and close the gap; planning comes after.

Q.04How do I choose an IT provider?

Get at least two written proposals and compare what each one includes, not just the monthly number. Ask who will do the work, what response times they commit to in writing, who holds your admin passwords and how you get everything back if you leave. CISA also recommends requiring multi-factor authentication and limiting the provider’s accounts to the systems they manage, without blanket administrator rights. Once you have the finalists, we can review their proposals side by side.

Q.05What should I ask before buying new business software?

Ask for the full yearly cost per user, including add-ons and the renewal price, plus the contract length and cancellation terms. Ask whether it supports multi-factor sign-in, how you export your data and in what format, and what happens to that data if you cancel. Then confirm it works with what you already use, such as your accounting, email or POS system, before anyone signs.

Q.06Can you look over a quote we got from another company?

Yes. Email it to info@manpasand.com or bring it to the free first conversation, and we’ll tell you what looks fair, what’s vague and what to ask before you sign. What you send us stays confidential. If the review grows into a bigger comparison, we’ll quote a fixed price for it before we start.
