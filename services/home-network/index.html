Home Wi‑Fi setup that fixes dead zones: placement, mesh or wired

Before we suggest buying anything, we look at where the router sits. A router on a closet floor at one end of the house often explains the weak bedroom at the other. Google’s advice for its Nest Wifi routers is to keep them off the ground, in the open and away from walls, doors and even aquariums. It also notes that thick brick or concrete walls hurt performance.

If moving the router isn’t enough, there are two main fixes. A mesh system is a set of matching units that relay your connection over Wi‑Fi. It needs no new wiring, but each wireless hop uses airtime your devices could have had. Google says its units work best no more than two rooms apart, and its sizing guide puts one on each floor of a two-story house. Wired access points, or mesh units joined by Ethernet (“wired backhaul”), skip that hop. TP-Link describes the result as lower latency with no bandwidth lost to a wireless hop.

Wired needs a cable path. About 83% of Rowlett’s housing units are detached single-family houses, and the median one was built in 1997 (Census Bureau, 2020–2024 American Community Survey), so wiring varies from house to house. We start by checking what’s already in the walls: Ethernet jacks, cable-TV coax or neither. Where there’s coax, MoCA adapters can carry the network over the existing cable.

One North Texas detail: mesh units are built for indoor rooms. Google rates its Nest Wifi Pro for dry, indoor spaces at 32–95°F. The National Weather Service puts DFW’s average first 100°F day at July 1 and the last at August 27. An attic or a closed-up garage isn’t air-conditioned, so we don’t hide a unit there to reach the backyard.

Locking down the router

Your router is the front door to everything else on the network. In May 2025 the FBI warned that criminals were taking over end-of-life routers with malware called TheMoon, which doesn’t need a password to get in. They used the routers as proxies to hide their own traffic. The FBI says routers from 2010 or earlier likely no longer get software updates. If yours is past end of life, replace it. On whatever router you end up with, we set these, following FTC and NSA home-network guidance:

  • A new admin password and network name in place of the factory defaults
  • WPA3 Personal, the newer and stronger Wi‑Fi encryption (or WPA2/WPA3 mixed mode if older devices can’t join), with a passphrase of at least 20 characters
  • WPS (push-button or PIN pairing), UPnP (lets devices open ports on the router by themselves) and remote management (changing router settings from outside the home) turned off. If a game console then can’t join online games, we open only the ports its maker lists, for that console alone.
  • Automatic firmware updates, where the router supports them
  • A weekly scheduled reboot, if the router can do it
  • A visible network name: hiding it adds no security and can cause connection problems

Separate networks for smart devices, guests and kids

The NSA’s home-network guidance says to split Wi‑Fi into at least three networks: main, guest and smart devices. That keeps the less secure gadgets from talking directly to your laptops and phones. Smart TVs, streaming sticks, speakers, thermostats, printers and game consoles go on the device network. Phones and computers stay on the main one, and visitors get guest Wi‑Fi.

The trade-off is convenience. Google says your phone has to be on the same Wi‑Fi network as a Chromecast to cast to it, and printers and speakers you reach from your phone can need the same treatment. So the TV you cast to every night may stay on the main network, or, if the router supports it, we let casting and printing cross between the networks. Either way, we test both from your phone before we call the job done.

Parental controls work best in layers. On the router we can set bedtime pauses and family filtering, such as Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 for Families, which can block malware and adult content. That covers the TV and console too. But router rules only see traffic on your Wi‑Fi, so they miss:

  • A phone on cellular data.
  • A device connected through a VPN.
  • Devices using a private (randomized) Wi‑Fi address, a privacy setting on newer phones; Google says its Family Wi‑Fi controls don’t work on them.

The FTC says controls need to be set up on every device your kid uses. So we pair the router rules with Apple, Google or Microsoft family settings on the kids’ own devices.

All of this is network work. We don’t install or service security cameras, video doorbells, smart locks or alarm sensors; the FAQ below explains why.

A home office that holds up on video calls

About 18% of Rowlett workers worked from home in the Census Bureau’s 2020–2024 American Community Survey, against about 14% statewide. For a work-from-home desk, the biggest upgrade is usually a cable. A laptop or dock plugged into Ethernet, or into a wired access point in the same room, doesn’t share airtime with the rest of the house.

The work computer goes on the main network, never the device network. If your employer manages the laptop, we leave its settings and VPN alone; the NSA points to a VPN as one way to reach work systems safely from home. If calls drop every time the lights flicker, a small battery backup for the modem and router helps, and our guide to protecting your tech from power outages covers sizing one. If the home office is a business with staff or customer records, it needs more than home gear: see network security for small businesses and our small business IT support.

Home network & Wi‑Fi security questions

All FAQs
Q.01Why is my Wi‑Fi slow in some rooms?

Usually it’s distance plus what’s in the way. Walls and floors weaken the signal, brick and concrete more than drywall, and the faster 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands lose range sooner than 2.4 GHz. A router tucked in a cabinet or on the floor makes it worse. Sometimes it’s the device: a phone or laptop without 6 GHz support can’t use that band at all, so we check both ends.

Q.02Do I need a mesh Wi‑Fi system?

Not always. A single, well-placed router can cover a smaller one-story home; Google, for example, rates one Nest Wifi Pro for up to 2,200 square feet. A two-story house, a long layout or brick walls inside often need more than one unit. If the house has Ethernet or coax in the right rooms, wired access points or wired mesh units usually do better than an all-wireless mesh.

Q.03Is the router from my internet provider good enough?

Often it’s fine for a smaller home, and your provider may push its updates automatically; the FTC suggests asking yours whether it does. The usual limits are coverage and how much you can change. The NSA suggests connecting a router you own to the provider’s equipment if you want more control over Wi‑Fi and security settings. We’ll tell you whether yours is worth keeping before you buy anything.

Q.04Should smart home devices be on a separate network?

Yes, in most homes. The NSA recommends at least three networks (main, guest and one for smart devices) so less secure gadgets can’t talk directly to your computers and phones. The catch is casting and printing from your phone, which need the right setup to work across networks. We set that up and test it before we leave.

Q.05How much does it cost to have someone set up home Wi‑Fi?

It depends on the house: its size, how many floors it has, what the walls are made of, whether there’s Ethernet or coax to use, how many devices you have and whether you need new equipment. We don’t publish prices, because two houses rarely need the same work. The consultation is free, and you get a fixed-price quote before any work starts, with any Texas sales tax shown as its own line.

Q.06Can you install my video doorbell or security cameras?

No. In Texas, installing or servicing security cameras, video doorbells, alarm sensors, smart locks or door-access systems for someone else requires a Private Security license from the Texas Department of Public Safety, and we don’t hold one, so we don’t do that work, camera wiring included. Hire a licensed company and contract with them directly; you can check any installer on DPS’s public license search. We look after the rest: the router, the Wi‑Fi and the devices you use every day.
